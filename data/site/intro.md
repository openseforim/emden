## Who was Jacob Emden?

Jacob Emden (1697–1776), who signed his name with the acronym *Ya'avetz*, was one of the most learned rabbis of
eighteenth-century Germany, and one of the most combative. He was born in Altona, beside Hamburg, the son of
[Ḥakham Tzvi Ashkenazi](names.html#hakham-tzvi) (d. 1718), a famous Talmudist who served as rabbi in the Three
Communities of Altona, Hamburg and Wandsbek and then in Amsterdam. Emden was for some years rabbi of the port town of
Emden in East Frisia, which gave him his name. He then went back to Altona, where he lived as a private scholar
without a rabbinical post, and printed his own books on his own press.

## The Eybeschütz controversy

In 1750 [Jonathan Eybeschütz](names.html#eybeschutz), one of the most admired Talmudists and preachers of the age,
became chief rabbi of the Three Communities. Early in 1751 Emden accused him in public of writing amulets that
contained hidden references to Sabbatai Zevi, the messianic claimant of 1665–66, whose secret followers were still
active. The quarrel split the communities, drew in rabbis all over Europe as well as the Danish crown (Altona was
then under Danish rule) and the Hamburg authorities, and for a time forced Emden to leave for Amsterdam. It went on
for years, and Emden wrote a long series of polemical books about it.

The quarrel did not come from nowhere. Emden's father had fought an earlier battle against a Sabbatian, the
kabbalist [Neḥemiah Ḥayon](names.html#hayon), in Amsterdam, and lost his post because of it. Emden saw his own fight
as the continuation of his father's.

## *Megillat Sefer*

*Megillat Sefer* ("The Scroll of a Book") is Emden's autobiography. It begins with his ancestors and the life of his
father (chapter 1), and then tells his own life (from chapter 2). It is one of very few Hebrew autobiographies of
its time, and it is unusually frank: Emden writes about his health, his marriages, his money troubles, and the
communal leaders, rivals and relatives he quarrelled with, often in bitter language. Emden did not publish it. It
survives in a manuscript copy in the Bodleian Library, Oxford (MS. Mich. 587; Neubauer 1723), which has not been digitized.

## Why Kahana's edition, and why Scholem's copy?

The historian David Kahana printed the text for the first time, in Warsaw in 1896/97, from the Oxford manuscript.
His edition is not complete. He left out passages that he took as repetitions, and he softened some of Emden's
language. A later edition (Bick, Jerusalem 1979) is no better: scholars reject it as unreliable. A critical edition
by Jacob J. Schacter has been announced but is not yet out.

The scan used here (HebrewBooks no. 37017) is the copy of Kahana's edition that belonged to Gershom Scholem
(1897–1982), the great historian of Kabbalah and of the Sabbatian movement. Its margins carry readings from the Oxford
manuscript that differ from Kahana's print. Most of them are harsh words about non-Jews, and curses on Eybeschütz and his
circle. So the copy shows a layer of censorship in Kahana's edition that the printed text alone does not show.

A handwritten key sheet in the copy (visible in the National Library of Israel's scan of it, not in the HebrewBooks
scan) explains the marks: the margin readings follow the Oxford manuscript; **V** marks an omission (the words are
missing in the print); **X** marks a substitution (the manuscript has the margin words instead of the printed ones); and
the copy was "compared with the manuscript by Ḥayyim Wirszubski", Scholem's student and colleague. Our reading of the
sheet is not certain in every word, but on this reading the collation is Wirszubski's, made in Scholem's copy.

This reader gives the margin readings where their mark points, marked <span class="sch sch-v">⟨S: …⟩</span> for an
addition (V) and <span class="sch sch-x">⟨S: …⟩</span> for a substitution (X), and lists them under each page with the
printed words they belong to and how certain the reading is.

## How to use this reader

- Each page of the 1896/97 edition is one section: the Hebrew, Kahana's footnotes, and the English.
- Use the bar at the top to show both languages or only one, to hide Kahana's notes, or to show the scan of the page.
- [Background for the reader](background.html): places, money, community offices, medicine, travel, printing and the Sabbatian quarrel.
- Names of people, places and books link to the [index](names.html). Bible and Talmud references link to Sefaria.
- The English translation is made with Claude (Anthropic). Each page has a "Report a mistake" link,
  which opens an issue on GitHub. Select words on the page first, and they go into the report.
