# Reading *Megillat Sefer*: background for the general reader

Jacob Emden (1697–1776) wrote for readers who shared his world. He does not explain what a *parnas* is, why a
rabbi needed a royal privilege to print books, or why bleeding from haemorrhoids was good news. This note gives the
background that a reader today is most likely to need. It follows the order of the topics, not of the book.

*Written 21-Nov-2026 with Claude (Anthropic). What it says about the book is checked against the
English translation (pp. 3–218).
Corrections are welcome on [GitHub](https://github.com/openseforim/emden/issues).*

## 1. Where and when

**The Three Communities (Altona, Hamburg, Wandsbek).** Most of the book takes place on the lower Elbe. Hamburg was a
free imperial city, governed by its own Senate, and Jews lived there on sufferance and on restricted terms. Altona,
just west of Hamburg's walls, belonged to the King of Denmark (as part of Holstein), and so did Wandsbek, a village to
the north-east. The Danish crown let Jews live and worship in Altona more freely than Hamburg did. From 1671 the
Ashkenazi Jews of the three places formed one joint community with one chief rabbi and one rabbinical court: in
Hebrew *ג' קהלות*, "the Three Communities", often abbreviated AHW. When Emden speaks of "the king", he usually means
the King of Denmark, whose officials in Altona could protect him or fail to.

**Amsterdam and the Sephardim.** The other centre of the book is Amsterdam. It had two Jewish communities: the rich,
long-established "Portuguese" (Sephardi) community, descended from converso refugees from Spain and Portugal, and the
poorer, larger Ashkenazi community from Germany and Poland. They had separate synagogues, rabbis and customs. Emden
admires the Sephardim's dignity and wealth (he tells the story of how they first settled in a free Holland) and blames
them bitterly for their part in the quarrel that drove his father out of the city in 1714.

**Central Europe.** Emden spent his twenties in Moravia (in Ungarisch Brod, now Uherský Brod in the Czech Republic,
his father-in-law's town) and travelled to Prague, Vienna, Breslau and Berlin. These were Habsburg and Prussian lands,
where Jewish residence depended on permits that could be withdrawn.

**War, plague, fire.** The early chapters run through the Great Northern War (1700–1721). In January 1713 Swedish
troops burned Altona, and Emden's family had already moved into Hamburg "until the fury of the men of war should
pass". Plague struck central Europe in the 1710s; Emden describes it reaching Brod in the first year of his marriage.
Fire, flood and the billeting of soldiers are ordinary dangers in the book.

## 2. Dates and the calendar

Emden dates events by the Jewish calendar: by the year from the creation of the world, by Hebrew months (Tishri,
Sivan, Shevat …), and by festivals and weekly Torah readings ("the Sabbath of the portion *Beshallaḥ*"). The
translation adds the civil year in brackets: 5511 [1751]. A Jewish year runs from autumn to autumn, so 5511 covers
the autumn of 1750 to the autumn of 1751. Hebrew writers often leave out the thousands, as in "the year [5]457
[1697]"; the abbreviation *לפ"ק* ("by the small count") marks this.

## 3. Money and what things cost

The usual units are the **Reichstaler** (the silver thaler of account of the Empire), the Hamburg **mark** (about
three marks to the taler), the gold **ducat**, and in Holland the **gulden**. Prices give a sense of scale better than
modern equivalents: Emden's father, a famous scholar, had only sixty Reichstaler a year as head of the Kloyz; a good
match brought a dowry of a thousand Reichstaler and several years' board; a prisoner for debt had to be fed at five
marks a week by his creditor. Large payments travelled as **bills of exchange** (*Wechselbriefe*), written orders on a
merchant in another city, which is why debts and journeys are so tangled in the book. Emden also tells of a relative
who won 28,000 gulden in the Utrecht lottery, and of his own heavy losses in dealing in lottery tickets and in jewels
and Brazilian diamonds.

## 4. The Jewish community and its offices

**Parnassim.** Each community was run by a small board of lay leaders, the *parnassim* (singular *parnas*), usually
rich merchants, often from a few families. They collected taxes for the community and the state, hired and paid the
rabbi, the cantor and the teachers, licensed residence, and could excommunicate. Many of the "villains" in the book are
parnassim; the margin readings in Scholem's copy (section 9) restore some of Emden's harshest words about them.

**Rabbi and court.** The chief rabbi was a salaried official engaged by contract for a term, and was also the *av bet
din*, "head of the court" (abbreviated אב"ד). His income came partly from fees, so disputes over salaries and fees
recur. A *gaon* is an honorific for a great Talmudist, not an office.

**The Kloyz.** A *kloyz* (Yiddish, from Latin *clausura*) was a private house of study endowed by a rich patron, where
a few scholars studied full time on stipends. Emden's father, Ḥakham Tzvi, headed the Kloyz of Altona for nearly
twenty years.

**Why "Ḥakham" Tzvi?** *Ḥakham*, "sage", was the Sephardi title for a rabbi. Tzvi Ashkenazi had studied in Salonika
among Sephardim and kept the title all his life, which is how an Ashkenazi rabbi came to be called "Ḥakham".

**Court Jews.** Some figures, such as Samson Wertheimer in Vienna, were financiers to princes, with influence at court
and wealth on a scale far beyond the ordinary community.

**Conversion.** Baptism was a way out of the community, and of its debts and discipline. Emden names converts and the
families they shamed; Kahana's edition left out many of these remarks.

## 5. Marriage, children and family

Marriages were arranged by parents, often between rabbinic families, with written terms: the dowry, and *kest*, years
of board in the father-in-law's house so that a young scholar could keep studying. Emden's marriage to the
granddaughter of R. Naftali Katz, and the move to Brod that came with it, is a model case. Women in the book are
mostly seen through their families, but some act strongly in money matters. Child mortality is constant: Emden lists
the children he lost, and illnesses such as smallpox and measles were part of every childhood.

## 6. Body and medicine

Emden is unusually frank about his body. His descriptions make sense within the **humoral medicine** of his day, in
which health was a balance of four fluids (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile).

- **"The black illness".** Melancholy, thought to come from black bile: low spirits, anxiety and bodily complaints
  together. Emden's father suffered from it after a financial loss.
- **"The golden vein".** The German *goldene Ader*: bleeding haemorrhoids. Doctors of the time thought such bleeding
  let out bad blood and relieved melancholy, so Emden thanks God when "the golden vein opened" for him.
- **Bleeding, enemas, purges.** The standard treatments; Emden takes "a treatment to open the bowels".
- **Hot water and tea.** Drinking large amounts of hot water and of tea was a fashionable cure, promoted by Dutch
  doctors in the late seventeenth century. Emden blames it for years of urinary trouble, switches to coffee, and is
  finally cured, as he sees it, by cold water in Amsterdam.
- **Spa cures.** His father was sent to "the bath of Ems" on the Lahn, near the Rhine, to drink and bathe in the warm
  springs.
- **Fevers.** "A shivering fever" on a sea voyage is probably malaria (ague), common in the Low Countries.

## 7. Travel

Distances are in leagues (German *Meilen*, several kilometres each). People travelled by hired wagon, by the slow
ordinary post, by expensive "special post" (Emden pays for 150 leagues of it from Brod to Hamburg), by river boat,
and by sea between Emden and Amsterdam. Journeys took weeks, involved border controls and Jewish body tolls, and were
dangerous in winter and in war.

## 8. Printing and books

Hebrew books could only be printed with a licence. Emden obtained a royal privilege from the King of Denmark for a
press in his own house in Altona (in the 1740s) and had to renew it when the king died and his son succeeded. Owning
a press made him independent: he printed his prayer book, his responsa and, above all, his polemics against Eybeschütz,
without needing the approval (*haskamah*) of other rabbis. Books are also property and evidence in the book: Emden
cites the pages of his own works, and a manuscript of Eybeschütz in Pressburg plays a part in the quarrel.

## 9. Sabbatianism, amulets and the great quarrel

**Sabbatai Zevi** (1626–1676) declared himself the Messiah in 1665 and set Jewish communities everywhere in turmoil;
in 1666 he converted to Islam. Some followers kept faith in him in secret, believing his apostasy was part of a hidden
plan. For rabbis like Emden and his father, crypto-Sabbatianism was a heresy hiding inside the community.

**The Ḥayon affair (1713–1714).** Neḥemiah Ḥayon, a kabbalist whose books Ḥakham Tzvi judged Sabbatian, was supported
by the Sephardi leaders of Amsterdam. The quarrel cost Ḥakham Tzvi his post; he left for London and Poland, and died in
Lemberg in 1718.

**Amulets.** A *kame'a* was a written charm, often for a woman in childbirth, made of divine names, letter
combinations and abbreviations. When amulets written by Jonathan Eybeschütz in Metz and Altona were opened, Emden read
in their letter-codes references to Sabbatai Zevi. Eybeschütz replied that the readings were forced. Whether he was a
secret Sabbatian is still debated. From 1751 the quarrel split the Three Communities and drew in rabbis across Europe,
the Danish court and the Hamburg authorities. Emden fled to Amsterdam in 1751 and came back two years later; the
fight lasted until Eybeschütz's death in 1764 and filled Emden's later life and the second half of this book.

**Curses.** Emden attaches curses to his enemies' names: "may his name be blotted out" (ימ"ש), "may the name of the
wicked rot" (שר"י). They are conventional formulas, but frequent and deliberate.

## 10. How the text reached us, and how to read this edition

Emden did not publish the book. It survives in one later copy in the Bodleian Library, Oxford (MS. Mich. 587); the
original is lost. David Kahana printed it in Warsaw in 1896/97. Kahana cut what he thought repetitive and softened
harsh language, especially about non-Jews and about Eybeschütz's circle. Gershom Scholem's copy of Kahana's edition
carries a collation with the Oxford manuscript in its margins, made (according to a key sheet in the copy) by Ḥayyim
Wirszubski: **V** marks words Kahana left out, **X** words he changed. In this translation those readings appear in
⟨S: …⟩ where their marks point, so a reader can see exactly what the censor removed.

Emden writes in a dense mosaic of biblical and rabbinic phrases (*melitzah*). The translation gives the verses in
standard wording with references, because the allusion often carries a joke, a curse or an irony. Kahana's own
footnotes, many identifying people from other sources such as Glückel of Hameln's memoirs, are translated under each
page.
