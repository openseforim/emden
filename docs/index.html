Jacob Emden, Megillat Sefer

Hebrew (Kahana, Warsaw 1896/97, from Gershom Scholem's copy) with an English draft translation · work in progress

Who was Jacob Emden?

Jacob Emden (1697–1776), who signed his name with the acronym Ya'avetz, was one of the most learned rabbis of eighteenth-century Germany, and one of the most combative. He was born in Altona, beside Hamburg, the son of Ḥakham Tzvi Ashkenazi (d. 1718), a famous Talmudist who served as rabbi in the Three Communities of Altona, Hamburg and Wandsbek and then in Amsterdam. Emden was for some years rabbi of the port town of Emden in East Frisia, which gave him his name. He then went back to Altona, where he lived as a private scholar without a rabbinical post, and printed his own books on his own press.

The Eybeschütz controversy

In 1750 Jonathan Eybeschütz, one of the most admired Talmudists and preachers of the age, became chief rabbi of the Three Communities. Early in 1751 Emden accused him in public of writing amulets that contained hidden references to Sabbatai Zevi, the messianic claimant of 1665–66, whose secret followers were still active. The quarrel split the communities, drew in rabbis all over Europe as well as the Danish crown (Altona was then under Danish rule) and the Hamburg authorities, and for a time forced Emden to leave for Amsterdam. It went on for years, and Emden wrote a long series of polemical books about it.

The quarrel did not come from nowhere. Emden's father had fought an earlier battle against a Sabbatian, the kabbalist Neḥemiah Ḥayon, in Amsterdam, and lost his post because of it. Emden saw his own fight as the continuation of his father's.

Megillat Sefer

Megillat Sefer ("The Scroll of a Book") is Emden's autobiography. It begins with his ancestors and the life of his father (chapter 1), and then tells his own life (from chapter 2). It is one of very few Hebrew autobiographies of its time, and it is unusually frank: Emden writes about his health, his marriages, his money troubles, and the communal leaders, rivals and relatives he quarrelled with, often in bitter language. Emden did not publish it. It survives in a manuscript copy in the Bodleian Library, Oxford (MS. Mich. 587; Neubauer 1723), which has not been digitized.

Why Kahana's edition, and why Scholem's copy?

The historian David Kahana printed the text for the first time, in Warsaw in 1896/97, from the Oxford manuscript. His edition is not complete. He left out passages that he took as repetitions, and he softened some of Emden's language. A later edition (Bick, Jerusalem 1979) is no better: scholars reject it as unreliable. A critical edition by Jacob J. Schacter has been announced but is not yet out.

The scan used here (HebrewBooks no. 37017) is the copy of Kahana's edition that belonged to Gershom Scholem (1897–1982), the great historian of Kabbalah and of the Sabbatian movement. Its margins carry readings from the Oxford manuscript that differ from Kahana's print. Most of them are harsh words about non-Jews, and curses on Eybeschütz and his circle. So the copy shows a layer of censorship in Kahana's edition that the printed text alone does not show.

A handwritten key sheet in the copy (visible in the National Library of Israel's scan of it, not in the HebrewBooks scan) explains the marks: the margin readings follow the Oxford manuscript; V marks an omission (the words are missing in the print); X marks a substitution (the manuscript has the margin words instead of the printed ones); and the copy was "compared with the manuscript by Ḥayyim Wirszubski", Scholem's student and colleague. Our reading of the sheet is not certain in every word, but on this reading the collation is Wirszubski's, made in Scholem's copy.

This reader gives the margin readings where their mark points, marked ⟨S: …⟩ for an addition (V) and ⟨S: …⟩ for a substitution (X), and lists them under each page with the printed words they belong to and how certain the reading is.

How to use this reader

  • Each page of the 1896/97 edition is one section: the Hebrew, Kahana's footnotes, and the English.
  • Use the bar at the top to show both languages or only one, to hide Kahana's notes, or to show the scan of the page.
  • Background for the reader: places, money, community offices, medicine, travel, printing and the Sabbatian quarrel.
  • Names of people, places and books link to the index. Bible and Talmud references link to Sefaria.
  • The English is a draft by Claude (Anthropic), not yet reviewed. Each page has a "Suggest a correction" link, which opens an issue on GitHub. Select words on the page first, and they go into the report.

Sources and method

Hebrew: David Kahana's edition (Warsaw 1896/97), from the HebrewBooks scan no. 37017, which is Gershom Scholem's copy (his library stamp is on scan p. 196). OCR by Kraken with the PP-OCRv6 model, then checked line by line against the scan by Claude. Running heads and page numbers are removed; Kahana's footnote calls are shown as small letters. The Pninim text of Bick's edition (Jerusalem 1979) was used only to find OCR errors.

English: draft translation by Claude (Anthropic), not yet reviewed. Biblical phrases are given in standard wording with the reference. Kahana's own footnotes are translated under each page.

Margin readings in Scholem's copy: by a handwritten key sheet in the copy (seen in the National Library of Israel's scan, IE12757688; not in the HebrewBooks scan), they follow the Oxford manuscript, V marks an omission and X a substitution, and the copy was compared with the manuscript by Ḥayyim Wirszubski. Our reading of the sheet is not certain in every word. The readings are shown as ⟨S: …⟩ (V, addition) or ⟨S: …⟩ (X, substitution) where their mark points, and listed with their certainty under each page; hover over a reading to see the printed words it belongs to. Where the type is not recorded the plain style ⟨S: …⟩ is used. Typed sheets bound into the copy (after pp. 8, 40 and 70) and a handwritten sheet (after p. 58) are given as "insert" sections. The HebrewBooks scan cuts off part of the right margin; the NLI scan of the same copy (258 images) shows the full margins and has not yet been used.

Not the manuscript: Kahana's text is not a full transcription of the Oxford manuscript (Bodleian, MS. Mich. 587; Neubauer 1723). A critical edition by Jacob J. Schacter has been announced.
