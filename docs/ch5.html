Once, too, when I hired a teacher to live in my house to teach my sons and daughters and to write, she was so angry that I was forced to put the man out of the house before his term was up, and to rent lodging for him until that man went mad — although in those days my livelihood was at hand without worry. Yet she used to do just this in the days of my poverty and my homeless wandering: while she was in Brod she kept a teacher for my first son alone, which was far beyond my means, and I agreed to her doing so; not only did I not put her to shame, I said on the contrary that this was good and pleasant. This was my way all my days; and even so she now stayed my hand and made me act against my judgement for the sake of peace in the house. Many such bitter needless quarrels happened with her; above all she was quarrelsome beyond measure with the maidservants, so that she was never at peace with them. When I bought the house I live in, too, she raised a great deal of strife and anger. Though I was almost weary of my life, "my sins turned these things to me" (cf. Jer. 5:25); for apart from this she was a worthy and sensible woman, also doing kindness with her own person and caring for the poor and the afflicted, and bringing up her sons to Torah in the times I stayed away from home, and running her house well, with care and cleanliness, her manservant and maidservant. She dealt in lending some years in Altona in a small way and succeeded: not only did I lose nothing, but there was blessing in it, as said above. Her one fault was anger, because she raged over nothing.

For our many sins, on the day of her death, the New Moon of Tammuz [5]499 [1739], she brought about my first vexation and quarrel with the parnassim and leaders of the holy community of Altona. For at the burial of the daughter mentioned, who died some years before her, they asked nothing of me beyond the custom at the burial of the townspeople, for they reckoned me as one of the townspeople, as I also bore the yoke of paying the tax they had laid on meat. But now they asked me for a pledge for her burial, and grief was added to my great pain and my sorrow over the death of the wife of my youth. I was forced to give them a pledge, so as not to delay her burial, and so that she might have peace in her last abode. In the year [5]498 [1738] I bought a fine dwelling house, and it too brought me envy and enmity. At first the man who lived in it stirred up a quarrel with me. Although I consulted him first and told him that if he wished to buy it I would not touch it — though I did not need to do so, for it belonged to a gentile and was sold publicly by auction — even so I did this beyond the letter of the law, and asked before several people whether he did not want it. A certain gentile had already bid a known sum for it, and they wanted to leave it with him. After that man assured me that it was too dear for him, and went and left it to the gentile, then I too made a bid on it and raised the price a little, and they told me: It is yours. After the purchase had been concluded with full force, the man turned round to make accusations against me, that I had taken his house, as though I had transgressed the law of the Torah, God forbid. But I had witnesses, and even the man's own sons testified against him and contradicted him; so he could not take it out of my hand.

I had great expenses on this house, for it was like a ruin when I bought it. I rescued it from the hand of a gentile in whose hand it had been for some years; a God-fearing Sephardi Jew had built it and fixed the Name in its walls, and by a miracle it had not been erased; but had it come into the hand of another gentile, it would certainly not have escaped erasure, God forbid. So I saw this too as a merit for me, especially as I set up in it a place for prayer too (although it is made on condition), and a room for my study. I spent several thousands on it, as if I had built it anew, and I made in it a ritual bath of rainwater (?); three times I had to repair it until it was "a plastered cistern" (m. Avot 2:8) holding its water. I also built a fine sukkah, with God's help, and a privy on the upper floor, and besides this many repairs inside and out. Eighteen years before this year [5]517 [1757] the holy society wanted to buy it and give me seven thousand Schock for it, and I wanted precisely a thousand gold pieces in gold; so the matter came to nothing. After this I spent much more on it in fine repairs and in strengthening its breaches, when I returned from Amsterdam, as I wrote above. My first wife lived in it three quarters of a year, and left me a nursing daughter, and I had to hire a wet-nurse for her. That daughter was healthy and good, and after some months she too died, for our many sins.

In those days when I was free, without a wife, I brought out into the light of print the book Iggeret Bikoret, which had been written and lying [by me] since the year [5]496 [1736], when I composed it; and in the year [5]499 [1739] I began to bring it to press. And immediately after I became a widower, many matchmakers spoke to me of honoured women on every side; but I did not set my eye on money. Now I will tell here wonderful things that are hard to believe; but "my witness is in heaven" (Job 16:19) that I do not change anything, only tell the matter as it happened, and show clearly that the words of the Sages, of blessed memory, are true, who said that it is decreed from heaven: this woman for this man; "and there is no counsel nor understanding against the Lord" [a]. And this is how it happened. Now I will return to the matter of the work of printing. If I said I would put into a book all that befell me because of it, "the bed would be too short to stretch oneself" (Isa. 28:20); for it would need a book of its own, and even so I would not reach a third or a quarter. Who could tell all the weariness that befell me in this business! I began with what I printed in my house through workmen, all of it my own, the book She'ilat Ya'avetz; I have already made known what happened to me with it. Afterwards, in the year after my third marriage, as said, I went to great expense to have new square letters made for me in Amsterdam, with vowel points and accents, which came to a large account, besides the expenses I had already had for tools and new Rashi letters, which together came to a large sum. And now I began the work of the Siddur, in the year [5]505 [1745], and I left other works, written and lying with me, and set my heart to take upon myself the yoke of the burden of this holy work, for it is great; especially as in this work I had not yet written anything of the service for the work of the Lord, and nothing had been written before at that time but the work Luaḥ Eresh, and that I left aside, so as not to provoke war with a man who is not of my craft; I made for it only markers and a record of references [a] in the work of the Siddur, which I called Ammudei Shamayim.

In it I included laws and customs that must be known for the whole year; also the order of the services that were practised in the time of the Temple, and all the events that befell our fathers, and the prophecies — on which day the prophets prophesied. All that is found in the Written and the Oral Torah that depends on time, I set in its place and on its day, for all the months of the year, and even hours for all the days of the week, fit for practice on the day itself, and the custom by night, on weekday and on holy day. I set everything apart by itself (cf. Gen. 21:29); I did not mix the matter of one day with another, for all the days of the year. All the prayers and the blessings, and the texts of the early supplications, I explained with God's help, and I explained them according to the four senses (PaRDeS), and customs, as far as I could bear. As a first undertaking it was a great, long and broad work, very heavy in quantity and quality, for I took upon myself even bringing the words of all the blessings in number, and to make for them "buds and blossoms" (cf. Ex. 25:33) in beautiful allusions. So too I set out in this work many plain senses, pleasant, sweet and lucid, both in difficult verses of Scripture, beyond the plain way, and in astonishing and strange sayings of the sages, of blessed memory, which became near and acceptable to the upright mind, with God's help.
